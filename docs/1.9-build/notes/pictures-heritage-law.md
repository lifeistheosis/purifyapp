# Pictures of monuments in Greece and Italy: what the heritage laws ask

Fable voices, 2026-11-11, on the arbiter's word. A research note for the
owner's decision. **Nothing in the app was changed for it.**

**What this is and is not.** It is information gathered from the sources
named below, read on the day. It is not legal advice, and three of its
questions can only be settled by a Greek and an Italian lawyer; they are
named at the end. Several primary texts could not be opened (the Greek
text of Law 4858/2021, Ministerial Decision 436630/2023, the fees decision
of 2011, the Italian decrees' own files), so where a statement rests on a
translation or on someone's summary, it says so.

## The question

Every picture named here is in the public domain as far as copyright goes:
the works are centuries old, and the photographs were taken from Wikimedia
Commons where they are marked public domain. That is what
`lib/saints/iconRights.ts` records, and it is true.

Greece and Italy each have a second kind of law, about cultural heritage
and not about copyright, which can ask for a permit and a fee to reproduce
pictures of monuments and museum objects, however old the work and whoever
took the photograph. Purify has a paid tier, so "commercial" may reach it.

## Greece: what the law asks, and of whom

**The statute.** Article 46 of Law 3028/2002 "On the Protection of
Antiquities and Cultural Heritage in General", now codified in Law
4858/2021 (the 2021 Greek text was not opened; this is WIPO's English
translation of the 2002 text). Paragraph 4: "A previous permission is
required for the production, reproduction and dissemination to the public,
for direct or indirect economic or commercial purpose" of depictions of
"monuments belonging to the Public Sector", of "immovable monuments that
are located in archaeological sites and historical places or are
isolated", and of "movable monuments that are kept in museums or
collections of the Public Sector", "in any way and by any means
whatsoever", "including electronic and digital means", "the Internet". The
permission is the Minister of Culture's, for a fee. Paragraph 5 allows use
"for other purposes, such as artistic, educational or scientific purposes"
for a fee, with exemptions possible.
https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/187586

**The procedure.** Ministerial Decision 436630/2023 (on the terms and the
procedure for permits to reproduce depictions of monuments belonging to the
Greek State) and the fees decision 126463/2011. Neither could be opened.
Wikimedia Commons' summary of them: putting photographs online is free
where there is no commercial or economic purpose, and "publishing on the
internet for commercial or economical purposes requires a license and the
payment of a fee to ODAP" (the Hellenic Organization of Cultural Resources
Development, which took over from the Archaeological Receipts Fund).
https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/Greece
A Greek law firm's note of 2025 says each commercial use needs its own
licence and fee, and each picture must name the monument and the Ministry.
https://www.ilnipinsider.com/2025/02/greek-monuments-in-advertising/

**Whose photograph it is does not matter.** The law speaks of reproducing
and spreading the picture, not of taking it. Commons marks such files with
a warning that reuse "may" need a further permit, and says such limits
"are not grounds for deletion" and "It is up to the reusers of
Commons-hosted media to ensure that they do not violate any non-copyright
restrictions".
https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Non-copyright_restrictions

**Who is asked.** The Ministry of Culture (its National Archive of
Monuments directorate publishes the forms, with one for internet use:
https://nam.culture.gr/en/?p=2457), with the fee to ODAP; for a state
museum's object, the museum; for the Rotunda, the Ephorate of Antiquities
of Thessaloniki City.

**What is not clear for most of the app's pictures.** Seventeen of the 25
wall paintings are on Mount Athos (counting St Peter of Alexandria's, of
1547, whose row names no monastery), in monasteries that own their
buildings, and ten of those are of 1547, after the date (1453) before
which the State holds monuments as its own. The decision's title and the
law firm speak of monuments "belonging to the Greek State". One source
says the law leaves the Athonite Charter's own rules in place
(https://athos.guide/en/athos-charter/athos-heritage-preservation-center).
Whether Article 46 reaches a monastery's fresco of 1547 was **not found**.
Hosios Loukas and Nea Moni are 11th-century monuments, older than 1453 and
on the World Heritage list, and are the likelier to be the State's in the
law's sense; the Veria museum's icon is a state museum's and is the
clearest case.

## Italy: what the law asks, and of whom

**The code.** The Codice dei beni culturali e del paesaggio (Legislative
Decree 42/2004). Article 107: the Ministry, the regions and other public
bodies "possono consentire la riproduzione" of the cultural property they
hold. Article 108: the body that holds the work sets a fee, weighing the
use and the applicant's gain; "Nessun canone è dovuto per le riproduzioni
richieste o eseguite da privati per uso personale o per motivi di studio";
and, since 2014 and 2017, "Sono in ogni caso libere le seguenti attività,
svolte senza scopo di lucro, per finalità di studio, ricerca", free
expression and making heritage known, including "la divulgazione con
qualsiasi mezzo delle immagini di beni culturali, legittimamente acquisite,
in modo da non poter essere ulteriormente riprodotte a scopo di lucro".
https://www.brocardi.it/codice-dei-beni-culturali-e-del-paesaggio/parte-seconda/titolo-ii/capo-i/sezione-ii/art107.html
https://brocardi.it/codice-dei-beni-culturali-e-del-paesaggio/parte-seconda/titolo-ii/capo-i/sezione-ii/art108.html

**The fees.** Ministerial Decree 161 of 11 April 2023 set minimum fees for
commercial reproduction of works held by state institutes; Decree 108 of
21 March 2024 replaced its table and, by press report, widened what is
free (scholarly and teaching publications, newspapers, open-access
publications), with commercial uses still paying.
https://www.finestresullarte.info/attualita/riproduzione-immagini-beni-culturali-cambia-disciplina-aumentano-gratuita

**Church property.** Articles 107 and 108 speak of property held by public
bodies. The two Ravenna mosaics are in churches. Italian dioceses run
their own permissions for commercial use of pictures of their works; their
force against someone who never entered the church is unclear.

**The cases.** Florence, 2023: Condé Nast was ordered to pay 50,000 euros
and a further 30,000 for using Michelangelo's David on a magazine cover.
Venice, 2022: Ravensburger was ordered to stop selling a puzzle of the
Vitruvian Man, the court claiming the code reaches beyond Italy. Stuttgart,
2024, upheld in June 2025: a German court held the Italian code cannot be
enforced outside Italy.
https://news.artnet.com/art-world/florence-gallerie-dellaccademia-wins-david-lawsuit-2313262
http://journals.law.harvard.edu/ilj/2025/04/two-pieces-of-one-puzzle-italy-and-germany-clash-over-the-transnational-reach-of-cultural-heritage-law-encroaching-upon-the-public-domain/
https://communia-association.org/2025/07/10/higher-regional-court-of-stuttgart-confirms-territoriality-of-italian-cultural-heritage-code/

**Who is asked.** The state museum or institute that holds the work; for
a church's, the diocese's office for cultural property.

## The realistic risk for an app with a paid tier

This is a reading of the sources, not advice.

- **No case was found against a website, an app, Wikimedia or a reuser of
  its files, in either country.** Every example of enforcement found is an
  advertisement, a magazine cover or merchandise by a sizeable company; in
  Italy the claimants were the particular state museums whose famous works
  were used. In Greece the one recent action reported was over a sportswear
  advertisement filmed at the Acropolis.
- **But neither law is limited to those uses.** Greece's reaches "direct
  or indirect economic or commercial purpose" and names the internet;
  Italy's free uses are those "senza scopo di lucro". A saint's small
  picture in a library that is free to read, inside a product that also
  sells a subscription, is not plainly non-commercial, and it is not
  plainly commercial either. That is the heart of the uncertainty.
- **The likeliest first contact, if any, is a letter asking for a permit
  and a fee, not a claim.** That is how both regimes are built. The cost of
  being wrong is then a fee per picture or taking a picture down; the two
  Italian judgments show it can be more where a famous work sells a
  product, which is not what the app does.
- **Reach.** Purify is run from the United States. The only foreign court
  to rule on the point refused to apply Italy's code abroad. No Greek
  equivalent was found. The app is offered in Greece and Italy and has
  Greek and Italian readers, so "abroad" is not a complete answer.
- **Where the exposure sits.** Least uncertain: the Veria museum's St
  Catherine (a state museum's object) and the Hosios Loukas and Nea Moni
  mosaics (six pictures and a cover). Most uncertain, and most numerous:
  the seventeen Athos frescoes, where the monasteries, not the State, may be
  the ones to ask, if anyone is. Italy: two Ravenna mosaics in churches.

## What cannot be settled without a lawyer

1. Whether Greece's Article 46 reaches works held by the Church and by the
   monasteries of Mount Athos, and frescoes painted after 1453.
2. What Decision 436630/2023 and the fees decision say in terms about
   internet use, and what a permit for a small picture costs.
3. Whether free reading inside an app with a paid tier is an "indirect
   commercial purpose" in Greece, or "a scopo di lucro" in Italy.
4. Whether either State would or could act against an operator in the
   United States.

## What the owner could choose

Set out for his decision; none is recommended as law.

- **Leave the pictures as they are and get a short opinion** from a Greek
  lawyer on questions 1 to 3 (most of the pictures are Greek), and act on
  it. The exposure meanwhile is, on what was found, a possible letter.
- **Ask for permits for the clearest cases only** (the Veria icon, the six
  mosaics of Hosios Loukas and Nea Moni), through the Ministry's internet
  form, and learn the real fee and the Ministry's own view of the rest from
  the answer.
- **Write to the monasteries** (Dionysiou, Stavronikita, the Protaton's
  keepers) as a courtesy as much as a duty, with the letters Admin is
  already writing for the modern icons. A monastery's blessing for its
  frescoes is worth having whatever the law says.
- **Replace the clearest cases** with pictures of works held elsewhere
  (many of these saints have old icons in Russia, Sinai, Serbia or in
  manuscripts), which removes the question for those and costs search
  time.
- **Keep the paid tier clear of them**: none of these pictures is sold or
  is a reason to subscribe, and saying so in the rights record would
  support the non-commercial reading. It would not settle it.
